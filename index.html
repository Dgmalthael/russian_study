Vowel Pairs: Hard vs. Soft

Russian vowels come in pairs. The "soft" vowel of each pair signals that the preceding consonant is palatalised (softened). This pairing is the engine behind almost every ending in the language.

Why this matters for cases
Endings exist in hard and soft versions of the same case. A hard-stem noun (стол) takes -ом in the instrumental; a soft-stem noun (музей) takes the soft mirror -ем. Learn one ending and you know its twin.

Stress & Vowel Reduction

Every Russian word has one stressed syllable, and its position is not predictable — it must be learned with the word. In this reference, stress is shown with an accent on key vocabulary: хорошо́, молоко́.

  • Unstressed о → "a": молоко́ is pronounced ma-la-KÓ.
  • Unstressed е/я → "i": язы́к sounds like yi-ZÝK.
  • Stress can move when a word declines or conjugates: окно́ → plural о́кна.

Grammatical GenderРод существительных

Every Russian noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter. Gender controls which endings the noun, its adjectives, and its verbs in the past tense will take — so it must be known for every noun.

The soft-sign problem — nouns ending in -ь
A noun ending in -ь can be either masculine or feminine, and the ending alone will not tell you. It must be memorised. слова́рь (dictionary) is masculine; две́рь (door) is feminine. Helpful tendency — but not a rule: most -ость nouns are feminine (но́вость news), and "month" words like апре́ль are masculine.

Natural gender wins over endings

Nouns naming male people are masculine even if they end in -а/-я: па́па (dad), мужчи́на (man), дя́дя (uncle) are all masculine and take masculine adjectives — but they decline like feminine nouns.

A small set of borrowed nouns are indeclinable and usually neuter: кофе (coffee — traditionally masculine), метро́, кино́, такси́, меню́. They never change form.

Common irregular plurals — learn these early
  • Stressed -а́ on some masculines: дом → дома́, го́род → города́, по́езд → поезда́, учи́тель → учителя́.

  • Fully suppletive: челове́к → лю́ди (person → people), ребёнок → де́ти (child → children).

Some nouns exist only in the plural (pluralia tantum): де́ньги (money), часы́ (clock/watch), очки́ (glasses), кани́кулы (school holidays).

Chapter 4 · A1

What Are Cases?Что такое падежи

A case is a change in the ending of a noun, adjective, or pronoun that shows the word's job in the sentence. English uses word order; Russian uses endings.

In English, "The dog sees the cat" and "The cat sees the dog" mean different things only because of word order. Russian instead changes the endings, so the words can move freely:

Соба́ка ви́дит ко́шку. sa-BÁ-ka VÍ-dit KÓSH-ku The dog sees the cat. — dog = subject (-а), cat = object (-у)
Ко́шку ви́дит соба́ка. KÓSH-ku VÍ-dit sa-BÁ-ka Same meaning — the dog still sees the cat. The endings carry the roles, not the order.

A memory order that helps
Russian schoolchildren learn the cases in the order Им–Род–Дат–Вин–Твор–Предл with a mnemonic. For learners it is often easier to study them by usefulness: Nominative → Accusative → Prepositional → Genitive → Dative → Instrumental, which is the order used in Part II.

What "declension" means

Declension (склоне́ние) is the act of running a noun, adjective, or pronoun through all six cases. A noun has 12 forms in total: 6 cases × 2 numbers (singular and plural). The good news: the patterns are highly regular, and three of the plural cases (dative, instrumental, prepositional) share one set of endings for all genders.

Chapter 5 · A1–A2

The Spelling RulesОрфографические правила

Three small spelling rules override the "expected" ending again and again. Master them now and most "irregular" endings stop being irregular.

Rule 1 — the "8-letter" spelling rule (most important)
After г к х  and  ж ч ш щ (and also ц),  write -и, never -ы.
→ кни́га + ы expected, but к forces кни́ги.   ру́чка → ру́чки, нож → ножи́.
Rule 2 — the hushing-consonant rule
After ж ч ш щ ц, write -а not -я, and -у not -ю.
→ This is why you never see "жя" or "щю" in Russian.
Rule 3 — the stress / o–e rule
After ж ч ш щ ц, write -о only when that syllable is stressed; write -е when it is unstressed.
→ instrumental: отцо́м (stressed -о) vs. ме́сяцем (unstressed -е).
Why you need this for the case tables
Every ending table in this reference gives the default form. Whenever a stem ends in one of these consonants, apply the rule above and the ending will "snap" to the legal version. This explains the bulk of what beginners mistake for irregularity.

When the Nominative Is Used

1 · The subject of the verb

The doer of the action stands in the nominative.

Студе́нт чита́ет. stu-DÉNT chi-TÁ-yet The student is reading.

2 · The predicate noun after "to be" (in the present)

Russian has no present-tense "is/are". Both halves of an "X is Y" sentence stay nominative.

Москва́ — столи́ца. mask-VÁ — sta-LÍ-tsa Moscow is a capital. (literally: "Moscow — capital")

3 · Naming, labels, and the dictionary citation form

Titles, list items, and the answer to "what is this?" (Что э́то?) use the nominative.

4 · The "have" construction names the possessed thing in the nominative

Russian expresses "to have" as "at someone there-is something". The owner goes in the genitive (Ch. 8), but the thing owned is the grammatical subject — nominative.

У меня́ есть маши́на. u mi-NYÁ yest ma-SHÍ-na I have a car. (lit. "at me there-is a car")

When the Accusative Is Used

1 · The direct object of a transitive verb

Я чита́ю кни́гу. ya chi-TÁ-yu KNÍ-gu I am reading a book.
Она́ лю́бит му́зыку. a-NÁ LYÚ-bit MÚ-zy-ku She loves music.

2 · Direction / destination — with в and на

With a verb of motion, в ("to/into") and на ("to/onto") take the accusative. Contrast this with location, which takes the prepositional (Ch. 11).

Я иду́ в шко́лу. ya i-DÚ f SHKÓ-lu I am going to school. (motion → accusative)
Я в шко́ле. ya f SHKÓ-lye I am at school. (location → prepositional)

3 · Duration and most time expressions

"For how long" and "on / every <day>" use the bare accusative.

Я ждал час. ya zhdal chas I waited for an hour.
Мы рабо́таем в сре́ду. my ra-BÓ-ta-yem f SRYÉ-du We work on Wednesday. (days of the week: в + accusative)

Prepositions That Take the Accusative

в into, to (motion) на onto, to (motion) за behind (motion); for под under (motion) про about че́рез across, in (time) сквозь through спустя́ after (time)

Most transitive verbs take a direct object in the accusative — this is the default.

The animacy rule — the heart of the accusative
The accusative ending depends on whether the noun is animate (a living being) or inanimate:
  • Inanimate masculine & all plurals of inanimates → look like the Nominative.
  • Animate masculine singular & all animate plurals → look like the Genitive.
  • Feminine -а/-я nouns have their own ending -у/-ю regardless of animacy.
  • Neuter nouns and feminine -ь nouns never change — accusative = nominative.

When the Genitive Is Used

1 · Possession and relation — "X of Y"

The owner / source word goes in the genitive and follows the thing possessed.

кни́га студе́нта KNÍ-ga stu-DÉN-ta the student's book (the book of the student)
це́нтр го́рода tsentr GÓ-ra-da the centre of the city

2 · Absence — нет, не было, не будет

To say something is not there, use нет + genitive. This also negates the "have" construction.

У меня́ нет вре́мени. u mi-NYÁ nyet VRYÉ-mi-ni I have no time. (lit. "at me there-is-no time")
Здесь нет магази́на. zdyes nyet ma-ga-ZÍ-na There is no shop here.

3 · Quantity, amounts, and numbers

  • After 2, 3, 4 (and 22, 23…): genitive singular — два стола́.
  • After 5 and above (and 0): genitive plural — пять столо́в.
  • After quantity words: мно́го воды́ (much water), ма́ло вре́мени, ско́лько де́нег?

4 · The partitive — "some of"

Да́йте воды́, пожа́луйста. DÁY-tye va-DÝ pa-ZHÁL-sta Give (me) some water, please.

5 · Dates — "the Nth of <month>"

"On what date" puts both the day and the month in the genitive: пе́рвого ма́я (on the first of May).

When the Dative Is Used

1 · The indirect object — the recipient

Я дал кни́гу дру́гу. ya dal KNÍ-gu DRÚ-gu I gave the book to (my) friend.
Она́ написа́ла письмо́ ма́тери. a-NÁ na-pi-SÁ-la pis-MÓ MÁ-ti-ri She wrote a letter to (her) mother.

2 · The experiencer in impersonal sentences

Feelings, states, age, and necessity are expressed with the person in the dative — they are not the grammatical subject.

Мне хо́лодно. mnye KHÓ-lad-na I am cold. (lit. "to-me [it is] cold")
Ему́ два́дцать лет. yi-MÚ DVÁ-tsat lyet He is twenty years old.
Нам на́до рабо́тать. nam NÁ-da ra-BÓ-tat We need to work.

3 · With нравиться — "to like"

Russian "to like" is built backwards: the liker is in the dative, the liked thing is the nominative subject.

Мне нра́вится э́тот фильм. mnye NRÁ-vit-sa É-tat film I like this film. (lit. "to-me this film is pleasing")

When the Instrumental Is Used

1 · The tool or means — no preposition

Я пишу́ ру́чкой. ya pi-SHÚ RÚCH-kay I write with a pen.
Он ест ло́жкой. on yest LÓSH-kay He eats with a spoon.

2 · Accompaniment — "together with", using с

Note the difference: with a preposition с it means "together with"; without a preposition the bare instrumental means "by means of".

Я иду́ с дру́гом. ya i-DÚ z DRÚ-gam I am going with a friend.
чай с са́харом chay s SÁ-kha-ram tea with sugar

3 · Professions and roles — after быть, стать, работать

"To be / become / work as" something puts that something in the instrumental (in the past, future, and infinitive — see the note below).

Он рабо́тает врачо́м. on ra-BÓ-ta-yet vra-CHÓM He works as a doctor.
Она́ хо́чет стать учи́телем. a-NÁ KHÓ-chit stat u-CHÍ-ti-lim She wants to become a teacher.

4 · Times of day and seasons

These adverbs are frozen instrumentals: у́тром (in the morning), днём (in the afternoon), ве́чером (in the evening), но́чью (at night); весно́й, ле́том, о́сенью, зимо́й (in spring/summer/autumn/winter).

"To be" — nominative vs. instrumental
Present tense (the "is" is silent): predicate is nominative — Он врач. Past, future, and infinitive: predicate goes instrumental — Он был врачо́м · Он бу́дет врачо́м · Он хо́чет быть врачо́м.

When the Prepositional Is Used

1 · Location — where something IS (в / на)

Contrast with the accusative of destination (Ch. 7): в/на + prepositional = static location.

Кни́га на столе́. KNÍ-ga na sta-LYÉ The book is on the table.

при Петре́ Пе́рвом pri pit-RYÉ PYÉR-vam under (in the era of) Peter the First

The number → noun case rule (essential)
The case of the counted noun depends on the last digit of the number:
  • 1 (and …21, …31) → nominative singular: оди́н стол, два́дцать оди́н стол.
  • 2, 3, 4 (and …22, …23, …24) → genitive singular: два стола́, три кни́ги, четы́ре окна́.

The Past Tense

The past tense is remarkably simple: it does not change for person. It changes only for the gender and number of the subject. Form it by removing -ть from the infinitive and adding:

Irregular pasts mostly involve verbs in -ти and -чь: идти́ → шёл, шла, шло, шли; мочь → мог, могла́, могло́, могли́; нести́ → нёс, несла́.

The Conditional MoodСослагательное наклонение

Russian expresses hypothetical and unreal situations — "would", "would have" — with one small, invariable particle: бы.

Formation

The conditional is simply the past tense + the particle бы. There are no special conditional verb forms at all. бы usually stands right after the verb or after the word it stresses, and may also appear as б after a vowel.

Я пошёл бы в кино́. ya pa-SHÓL by f ki-NÓ I would go to the cinema.
Без тебя́ я не смог бы э́то сде́лать. byes ti-BYÁ ya ni SMOK by É-ta SDYÉ-lat Without you I couldn't have done this.

Real vs. Unreal Conditions

  • Real condition ("if X happens, Y will happen") — no бы; use е́сли + ordinary tenses: Е́сли бу́дет дождь, я оста́нусь до́ма.
  • Unreal / hypothetical ("if X were…, Y would…") — бы appears in both clauses: Е́сли бы у меня́ бы́ло вре́мя, я бы помо́г. — "If I had time, I would help."
бы also forms polite wishes and softened requests
Я хоте́л бы… — "I would like…" (softer than хочу́).   Хорошо́ бы… — "It would be nice if…".   чтобы (= что + бы) introduces purpose and indirect commands — see Chapter 28.

What -ся Does

  • True reflexive — the action turns back on the subject: мыть → мы́ться (to wash oneself), одева́ться (to dress oneself).
  • Reciprocal — "each other": встреча́ться (to meet one another), céловаться (to kiss).
  • Intransitive / change of state — начина́ться (to begin), конча́ться (to end), открыва́ться (to open).
  • Passive — with imperfective verbs: Дом стро́ится рабо́чими — "The house is being built by workers."

A reflexive verb cannot take a direct object
Because -ся historically is the object ("self"), a verb with -ся never takes an accusative direct object. Its complements come in other cases: занима́ться + instrumental, боя́ться + genitive, учи́ться + dative.

Chapter 27 · B1–C1

Word Order & SyntaxПорядок слов

Because cases mark grammatical roles, Russian word order is free to do a different job: managing the flow of information — what is old news and what is new.

Theme & Rheme

The neutral pattern places known information first (the theme) and new, important information last (the rheme). The end of the sentence is the natural focus position.

Анна купи́ла маши́ну. ÁN-na ku-PÍ-la ma-SHÍ-nu Anna bought a CAR. — answers "What did Anna buy?"
Маши́ну купи́ла Анна. ma-SHÍ-nu ku-PÍ-la ÁN-na It was ANNA who bought the car. — answers "Who bought the car?"

Both sentences are fully grammatical and mean the same event — the cases (Анна nom., маши́ну acc.) keep the roles fixed. Only the emphasis changes.

Free, but not random
Word order is flexible, not arbitrary. Neutral order is Subject–Verb–Object. Moving a word to the end highlights it as new information; moving it to the front marks it as the topic. In speech, intonation does similar work. Adjectives normally precede their noun; не stands directly before the word it negates.

Questions

A yes/no question can have the same word order as a statement — only the rising intonation (or, in writing, the question mark) marks it: Вы говори́те по-ру́сски? Question words (кто, что, где, когда́, почему́, как) come first. The particle ли adds a formal yes/no question: Зна́ете ли вы…?

Same logic — antecedent кни́га (feminine)

кни́га, кото́рая лежи́т на столе́ the book which is lying on the table — Nom. fem. sg.
кни́га, кото́рую я чита́ю the book (which) I am reading — Acc. fem. sg.
кни́га, кото́рой у меня́ нет the book which I don't have — Gen. (after нет)
кни́га, в кото́рой мно́го карти́н the book in which there are many pictures — Prep. (after в)

Plural antecedent — лю́ди

лю́ди, кото́рые живу́т здесь the people who live here — Nom. pl.
лю́ди, кото́рых я ви́жу the people whom I see — Acc.=Gen. (animate pl.)
друзья́, с кото́рыми я живу́ friends with whom I live — Ins. pl.
The 3-step procedure for picking the right form
  1. Find the antecedent (the noun in front) → fixes gender and number.
  2. Inside the relative clause, ask: what job does "which / who" have? (subject, direct object, after a preposition, indirect object…) → fixes the case.
  3. Combine (1) and (2) and read off the form from the table above.
Common mistakes to avoid
  • Word order: кото́рый normally comes first inside the clause, but a preposition stands in front of it: …студе́нт, с кото́рым я говори́л — never *который с…*
  • Don't use это instead of который: "the book which I read" is кни́га, кото́рую я чита́л, never *кни́га, э́то я чита́л*.
  • Comma is obligatory before который — Russian punctuation is rule-based.
  • чей ("whose") is a separate possessive relative — see below.

Sister relatives: кто, что, чей, где, куда́, отку́да, когда́

When the antecedent is itself a pronoun or missing, you use кто / что (often paired with тот / то / все):

Те, кто опозда́л, оста́лись без обе́да. Those who were late were left without lunch. — кто after те.
Всё, что ты сказа́л, — пра́вда. Everything (that) you said is true. — что after всё.

чей, чья, чьё, чьи ("whose") declines like a soft adjective and shows possession in the relative clause:

челове́к, чей сын у́чится в МГУ a person whose son studies at Moscow State University

Place & time relatives где (where, at), куда́ (where, to), отку́да (where from), когда́ (when) often replace clumsy в кото́ром / в кото́рое вре́мя:

го́род, где я роди́лся the city where I was born (= в кото́ром)
день, когда́ мы встре́тились the day when we met

Indirect Speech

Russian indirect speech does not shift tenses the way English does. The tense of the original utterance is kept:

Он сказа́л, что он рабо́тает. He said that he was working. — Russian keeps the present рабо́тает; English shifts back.

Reported yes/no questions use the particle ли: Он спроси́л, рабо́таю ли я — "He asked whether I work."

Word FormationСловообразование

Russian builds large families of words from a single root using prefixes and suffixes. Recognising the pieces lets you decode — and predict — vocabulary you have never seen.

The Anatomy of a Word

A word is built from a root (ко́рень) plus optional prefixes (приста́вка), suffixes (су́ффикс), and an ending (оконча́ние). From the root -уч-/-ук- ("learn"): учи́ть, учи́ться, учи́тель, учени́к, нау́ка, учёный, изуча́ть.

  • Agent nouns — -тель, -ник, -ист: писа́тель (writer), учени́к (pupil), тури́ст.
  • Abstract nouns — -ость, -ение, -ство: мо́лодость (youth), образова́ние (education), бога́тство (wealth).
  • Diminutives / affection — -ик, -ок, -очк-, -еньк-: дом → до́мик, кни́га → кни́жечка, ма́ма → ма́мочка.

Why this is worth it
Diminutives are not just "small" — they carry warmth, informality, or politeness, and they pervade everyday speech. And once you can spot a root, an unfamiliar word like переучи́ваться decodes itself: пере- (re-) + -уч- (learn) + -ся → "to retrain".
Chapter 30 · B1–C1

Impersonal Constructions & NegationБезличные конструкции и отрицание

Two areas where Russian logic differs sharply from English: sentences with no grammatical subject, and the way Russian piles up negatives.

Impersonal Constructions

Many Russian sentences have no subject in the nominative at all. The verb stays in a fixed neuter/3rd-person form, and the person involved — if any — appears in the dative.

Мне хо́лодно. I am cold. (lit. "to-me [it is] cold")
Здесь нельзя́ кури́ть. Smoking is not allowed here.
Вечере́ет. Evening is drawing in. (weather/nature — no subject possible)
Нам на́до идти́. We have to go.

Key impersonal predicate words: мо́жно (one may / it is possible), нельзя́ (one may not / it is impossible), на́до · ну́жно (it is necessary), тру́дно, легко́, ва́жно, по́здно, пора́.

The Negative нет + Genitive

To state that something is absent, Russian uses нет (present), не́ было (past), не бу́дет (future) — each followed by the genitive. The neuter past не́ было and future не бу́дет never change for the "missing" thing.

В го́роде нет метро́. There is no metro in the city.
Вчера́ его́ не́ было до́ма. He wasn't at home yesterday.

Double (Multiple) Negation

Russian REQUIRES the double negative
Where English allows only one negative ("I see nothing"), Russian demands that a negative pronoun/adverb and the verb both be negated. The more negatives, the more grammatical the sentence.
Я ничего́ не зна́ю. I don't know anything. (lit. "I nothing not know")
Никто́ никогда́ ничего́ не говори́л. Nobody ever said anything. — four negatives, all obligatory.

не vs. ни

  • не — the basic negator; placed directly before the word it negates: не зна́ю, не сего́дня, не он.
  • ни — intensifies an existing negation and forms negative pronouns (никто́, ничто́, никогда́, нигде́). In ни… ни… it means "neither… nor…".
Genitive of negation
The direct object of a negated verb very often shifts from accusative to genitive, especially with abstract nouns: Я не зна́ю отве́та (gen.) — "I don't know the answer." With concrete, specific objects the accusative is still common — both occur, and the genitive sounds more categorical.

The fluency formula
Fluent speakers don't know more grammar — they have hundreds of memorised chunks ready to use without composing them on the fly. Learn whole phrases, not single words. Saying приня́ть реше́ние instead of guessing "to make + decision" is what separates B1 from B2.
